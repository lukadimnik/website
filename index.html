I lead engineering teams, and the part I actually care about is whether the people on them are doing the best work of their careers — and enjoying it. My philosophy is simple: happy engineers are productive engineers. I've seen what happens when that's true: a team that holds together through a rough pivot instead of scattering, people who tell you — unprompted — that this is the best job they've had. That's the number I actually track, even if it doesn't fit on a dashboard.

I'm a hands-on EM, which mostly means I've refused to become a full-time meeting attendee. I still write code, I run agentic development workflows with Claude Code daily, and I've authored internal Claude skills my team uses for codebase onboarding. I stay close enough to the work to make good technical calls and clear obstacles fast, without getting in the team's way.

What actually keeps me here is simpler than any of the job titles: I like to tinker. I like the moment a tangled system — or a tangled team — finally clicks into something you can reason about. I like deleting a workaround. I learn by building, breaking, and rebuilding, which is also why I run a Kubernetes homelab in my spare time, and pair agentic workflows with real infrastructure to reverse-engineer decade-old codebases nobody's touched in years.

Ship things that work

I'd rather deliver something solid and real than something perfect and theoretical. Working software, in front of people, beats a beautiful plan every time.

Untangle the mess

I'm drawn to the gnarly, undocumented, tightly-coupled financial systems nobody wants to open — invoicing, ledgers, settlement flows — and to the teams going through a rough patch. Making both legible again is the part I genuinely enjoy.

03

Stay hands-on

Titles change; the urge to tinker doesn't. I lead best when I'm still close to the code, the infra, and the people doing the work.
